Why an Espresso Lever

I'm a coffee addict and appreciator of it — as is the rest of my family.

Away at school, the thing I missed most about home was good coffee. My kitchen had a Keurig drip maker and not much else, and I was spending an embarrassing amount of money at cafés. So for my ME103 final project, I decided to build the thing I actually wanted: a machine that lets me pull my own espresso, by hand.

The concept is an old one — a manual lever. You load ground coffee into a portafilter, add hot water to the chamber, and press down on a long lever arm. That leverage drives a sealed piston into the chamber and forces the water through the coffee puck. The reward is a real, freshly pulled shot with zero electronics involved.

The catch: unlike my earlier projects that were mostly 3D-printed and laser-cut, this one had to be machined from solid metal and built around real, purchased espresso parts. That changed everything.

02

Designing Around Real Parts

In ME102, most parts were things I designed and printed from scratch. This project flipped that. Before I could design anything, I had to research and buy the parts the machine would live around — the portafilter, the shower screen and gasket, and an o-ring set for the piston seal — and then design every machined part to fit them. The hard part of a mechanism, I learned, isn't figuring out how it should work. It's making all the real pieces actually work together.

The rabbit hole
A Million O-Rings

Pistons and o-rings were far less intuitive than I expected. Open one supplier page and you're staring at every cross-section and material imaginable. What inner diameter should the chamber be? How much clearance does the piston need? Should the piston be smaller than the portafilter? Start from a solid rod, or a tube? Each choice felt small on its own.

The realization
Everything Is Coupled

Rethinking one dimension meant rethinking three others. The design phase became a constant back-and-forth between diameters, clearances, and pivot positions until the whole thing was finally cohesive. This is where I learned to think past “how it works” and into “how to actually make it work” — weighing real trade-offs instead of ideal ones.

03

CAD & Mechanism

I leaned on my CAD harder than on any project before. With so many interdependent dimensions, an accurate model was the only way to be sure the mechanism would fully open without the piston getting stuck, reach the bottom of the chamber on the down-stroke, and keep every pivot point spaced correctly. I used section analysis to watch the piston travel through the chamber before cutting a single chip of metal.

Prototyping the Mechanism

Before committing to metal, I worked the geometry out in foam and quick mock-ups. The highest-risk element was always the piston–cylinder subsystem: could I make it seal air-tight, brew without leaking, and still be easy to assemble? Cheap prototypes let me answer the geometry questions first.

Foam study 1
Where the Hinge Goes

Foam let me feel out the distance between the base–lever hinge and the cylinder — the single choice that sets how the whole mechanism moves.

Too close → not enough freedom of motion for a full stroke

Fabrication & Machining

This was my first real machining project, and it's where the design met reality. Every metal part was turned on the lathe or cut on the mill from solid 6061 aluminum. I had a plan for how to make each part — but with no intuition yet for how a drawing becomes a physical, clamped, chip-making operation, plenty of details only revealed themselves once I was standing at the machine.

I planned every session before I walked into the lab. With limited time and limited stock, I couldn't afford to improvise a setup and hope — I had to be confident in each operation and each way of holding the part before I started cutting.

Lathe
Chamber / cylinder. Faced and turned the outer diameter from bar stock, then bored the inner diameter to fit the piston with the right clearance, parting and re-mounting to reach both faces.
Lathe
Piston. Turned to diameter and cut the two o-ring grooves with a grooving tool, stepping over in small increments until each groove hit its target width and depth for the seal.
Mill + rotary table
Internal grooves. The seats for the portafilter and gasket couldn't be cut with the lathe tooling I had, so I moved the chamber to the rotary table on the mill to machine those internal features.
Mill
Handle slots. The lever needed two parallel side slots — but they can't be cut in one setup because there's no clean way to clamp for both at once. I had to indicate the part in twice and machine each side independently while keeping them true to each other.
Bench
Finishing. Deburring, filing the frame cutout, and fitting the purchased parts — portafilter, shower screen, gasket, and o-rings — until everything seated and sealed.

Challenges & Fixes

Problem
Slots That Can't Be Clamped

The handle needs two slots on opposite sides that have to end up parallel — but there's no good way to clamp the part to cut both in a single setup.

Fix

I re-indicated and re-clamped the part between operations, machining each slot on its own while carefully referencing the same datums so the two stayed true to each other.

Problem
Grooves the Lathe Couldn't Cut

I'd planned to cut the chamber's internal grooves — where the portafilter seats and the gasket sits — on the lathe. The tooling we had simply couldn't reach or form them.

Fix

I re-thought the setup on the fly and moved the chamber onto the rotary table on the mill, which let me machine the internal features a different way. Limited time meant I had to be certain before committing.

Risk
An Air-Tight Piston Seal

The whole machine lives or dies on the piston sealing against the chamber — leak and there's no pressure, and no espresso.

Fix

A two-groove o-ring layout, dimensioned in CAD and dialed in on the lathe, gave a seal that holds pressure while still sliding smoothly through the bore.

The crunch
The Last Two Weeks

Deadline pressure, long lab sessions, and constant troubleshooting blurred together. There were moments I genuinely doubted I'd finish in time.

The payoff

Watching the parts get fabricated and slowly come together was thrilling — and pulling the first real shot made every late night worth it.

Final Remarks

Designing to make it work

Coming in, I thought the hard part of a mechanism was the design itself. What I actually learned is that the tough part is bringing it together. Unlike past projects where parts were printed or laser-cut, this one meant researching purchased parts and designing around them — and every small choice about a diameter, a clearance, or a pivot rippled into three others. I developed the skill of thinking past how a design is supposed to work and into how to genuinely make it work, weighing real options against real constraints.

Learning to machine

I'd wanted to learn to machine for a long time, and this project finally taught me — not just the tools, but how engineering actually happens in the shop. I had no intuition for how a plan translates into a physical setup, so problems surfaced that I hadn't thought about: slots that can't be clamped, grooves the lathe couldn't cut. With limited time and stock, I couldn't just try a setup on a whim; I had to plan each session in advance and be confident in every operation. That's where I got creative and learned to troubleshoot on my feet.

The hardest — and most rewarding

The final stretch nearly broke me: the deadline pressure and endless troubleshooting had me feeling a little crazy. But seeing the parts come together and actually work was incredibly rewarding. More than any single skill, I came away with a real sense of how much deliberate, behind-the-scenes work ties a product together. This was the hardest and most rewarding project I've ever made — and next, I'd love to refine the one-way-valve piston so it re-fills on the up-stroke and pulls back-to-back shots.

Alexa Cavazos

Stanford University · Mechanical Engineering
